In what year does Wilderhold take place do you think is the best for our game's story and lore?

For Wilderhold, I’d set the main game in 2377 CE — but almost nobody in the world still uses the old calendar.
In-world, it would be known as something like Year 311 A.W. — After the Wilding.
That gives us a fantastic lore foundation: the Wilding occurred in 2066, when whatever combination of ecological collapse, ancient forces, elemental upheaval, dimensional rupture, or human experimentation permanently changed the world. Three centuries later, humanity remembers the old civilization mostly through ruins, relics, corrupted machinery, legends, blueprints, and things people no longer fully understand.
Why 2377 works so well
It creates the exact technological/fantasy mixture Wilderhold seems to want.
Old-world technology can exist without making the world feel like a modern shooter. Guns, ammunition, machinery, reactors, industrial facilities, underground complexes, vehicles, and strange technology can all be archaeological treasures rather than everyday conveniences.
Meanwhile, 311 years is enough time for an entirely new civilization to emerge. Nobody is simply "surviving the apocalypse" anymore. The apocalypse is ancient history.
People have built:
Holds and fortified settlements
Chieftain cultures
New religions and superstitions
Crafting traditions
Elemental disciplines
Wildlife mythology
Trade routes
Scavenger guilds
Tower-building knowledge
Completely new political borders
And nature has had centuries to reclaim everything.
That matters enormously for the identity of Wilderhold.
The world shouldn't feel like:
"Civilization collapsed recently."
It should feel like:
"Civilization collapsed so long ago that the wilderness became civilization."
That is much stronger.
The calendar I would use
Old Calendar: 2377 CE
Common Calendar: 311 A.W.
A.W.: After the Wilding
Scholars might still know the old dates, but regular people could say things like:
"The Rust Crown fell in 284 A.W."
"Glassborn were first recorded during the Long Frost of 167 A.W."
"No expedition has crossed the northern Wilds since 303."
And archaeological items could occasionally contain dates such as:
08/17/2059
which would immediately tell the player:
"Holy shit. This thing is more than three hundred years old."
That contrast would be fantastic environmental storytelling.
What exactly happened in 2066?
I would not reveal the full answer immediately.
Call the event The Wilding.
Before it, humanity had reached a technological peak. Then several apparently unrelated catastrophes occurred almost simultaneously.
The seas changed.
Weather systems destabilized.
Massive sections of land fractured.
Previously unknown organisms appeared.
Animals began displaying impossible adaptations.
Certain regions developed strange elemental phenomena.
And something beneath the Earth—or perhaps something that had always been there—woke up.
Human civilization collapsed.
But here's the important twist:
Humanity initially believed the Wilding destroyed the world.
Over the course of Wilderhold, players gradually discover that this isn't quite true.
The Wilding changed the rules of the world.
Magic, elemental forces, mutated wildlife, bizarre materials, relic technology, your disciplines, and perhaps even some bosses could all ultimately trace back to the same underlying phenomenon.
That gives the entire game a unified mythology rather than separate "fantasy stuff" and "sci-fi stuff."
And the name Wilderhold suddenly becomes literal.
A Hold is one of humanity's fortified islands of civilization.
Everything beyond it is the Wilder.
People might commonly say:
"Beyond the Hold, the world belongs to the Wilder."
And eventually the player learns that humanity isn't necessarily supposed to conquer the Wilder.
The deeper thematic question becomes:
Are humans rebuilding civilization—or repeating the mistake that caused the Wilding?
That gives Wilderhold a surprisingly deep story underneath all the towers, loot, extraction, companions, crafting, waves, bosses, and progression.
If we establish 2377 CE / 311 A.W. as canon, I think we could build the entire Wilderhold timeline next—from the world before 2066, through The Wilding, the founding of the first Holds, the origin of each enemy faction, all the way to Act 1, Day 1. That could become the backbone for virtually every biome, boss, relic, discipline, and future expansion.

WILDERHOLD — FINAL CANON STORY
The central idea
Wilderhold is fundamentally a story about movement versus control.
The world was never meant to stand still.
Before kingdoms, borders, roads, or maps, enormous living beings called Worldstriders wandered across the land. Mountains of flesh and stone, they lived for centuries. Forests grew across their backs. Rain collected in valleys of their hides. Animals nested there. Eventually people climbed onto them.
And stayed.
Human civilization did not originally conquer the land.
It traveled with it.
Generations were born, lived, and died aboard Worldstriders. Towns moved across continents without wheels. People measured distance in migrations rather than miles.
Where a Worldstrider walked, life followed.
This era became known centuries later as the Walking Age.
And then one man decided that the world would be safer if it stopped moving.
His name was Kharok.
THE WALKING AGE
The Worldstriders followed invisible currents running through the earth called the Roads.
People eventually discovered that the Roads were not merely paths.
They were power.
Everything living touched them.
Fire, water, stone and wind were different expressions of the same underlying force. Plants grew differently where Roads crossed. Wildlife changed across generations. Storms followed certain routes. Migration, weather, fertility and even instinct seemed connected to them.
Worldstriders could sense these currents naturally.
They carried that power with them.
This phenomenon eventually became known as Roadsong.
It is the foundation underneath virtually every supernatural system in Wilderhold.
Elemental towers manipulate Roadsong.
Magic disciplines manipulate Roadsong.
Relics contain crystallized Roadsong.
Certain wildlife can sense it.
Portents are glimpses of where Roadsong may flow next.
And Worldstriders are effectively enormous living conductors of it.
That gives Wilderhold one underlying mythology instead of having “magic,” “towers,” “wildlife,” “relics,” and “Worldstriders” exist as disconnected fantasy concepts.
They all come from the same thing.
KHAROK BEFORE THE CHAINMAKER
Kharok was not born a tyrant.
This matters tremendously.
He was once a Warden.
In fact, tradition remembers him as one of the greatest Wardens who ever lived.
A Warden's duty was simple:
One defender. One Worldstrider.
The Warden protected the settlements upon their beast, rode ahead when the road became dangerous, negotiated passage with other wandering peoples, defended the creature against hunters, and protected the people when migrations crossed hostile territory.
Kharok believed deeply in that purpose.
Then came a catastrophe remembered as the Breaking Migration.
Several Worldstriders changed course simultaneously.
Nobody knew why.
Roadsong shifted.
Rivers moved.
Seasonal routes failed.
Settlements that expected fertile valleys arrived instead at drought.
Two Worldstriders collided during a migration.
Another collapsed.
Thousands died.
Kharok's own Worldstrider was mortally wounded.
He could not accept that something so enormous—and so many lives depending upon it—could be destroyed simply because the world decided to change direction.
So Kharok searched for a way to make the Roads predictable.
And at the summit later called the Crown of the World, he discovered how.
THE FIRST CHAIN
Kharok learned that Roadsong could be held.
Not destroyed.
Not redirected.
Held.
He forged enormous runic anchors and drove them into the earth.
Then he connected them through a form of magic nobody had previously attempted.
Chain-magic.
The first chain was intended to save his dying Worldstrider.
And it worked.
The creature stopped.
Its wounds stabilized.
The land around it became predictable.
Rain returned reliably.
Fields could be planted permanently.
Buildings no longer needed to survive migration.
For the first time in human history, people could confidently say:
We will still be here next year.

Kharok was celebrated.
Other settlements demanded the same miracle.
Another Worldstrider was anchored.
Then another.
Permanent roads appeared between them.
Cities followed.
Trade flourished.
Maps finally stayed accurate.
The Walking Age ended not through conquest—
but because stability was wonderful.
That is the tragedy at the center of Wilderhold.
The Chain began as civilization's greatest achievement.
THE PRICE OF STILLNESS
Worldstriders were never supposed to stop.
Roadsong began pooling around the anchors.
Forests changed.
Water stagnated.
Certain animals became unnaturally aggressive.
Elemental phenomena appeared.

The world wasn't dying.

Kharok faced an impossible decision.
Release the Worldstriders and admit that the permanent civilization humanity had built upon the chains could collapse—

Worldstriders resisted them.
People resisted them.

A command could be carried through the chain.

Then an impulse.

The Chainbound Host

The old Wilderhold Chronicle:
There shall come a day when the Earth itself answers mankind. Pray we understand the answer better than those before us.

And the most famous one:
When every road is bound, the Earth will speak.

Nobody knows these stories originated from an actual event.
Until Act 8 or 9.
The Ashfen revelation
The Ashfen should preserve a damaged artifact.
Perhaps a metal tablet copied countless times.
Its original words are almost meaningless to them:
EARTH SYSTEMS WITNESS ARRAY
BIOSPHERE INTEGRITY AUTHORITY
PRIMARY INTERFACE OFFLINE
But one word remains readable:
WITNESS
The Ashfen call the legendary creature:
The Witness Beneath.
This becomes the first major clue that their folklore may describe something real.
The Hollow Marches
The fog can contain another clue.
The Warden discovers a shaft disappearing far underground.
It is far too large to be a mine.
Along its walls are broken conduits.
Something enormous once passed through here.
Not upward.
Downward.
But the player begins realizing:
This was infrastructure.
Rustwood
The microorganism network beneath Rustwood is accidentally connected to remnants of the Earthwitness.
That is why the forest sometimes behaves almost intelligently.
Trees respond to disturbances.
Roots grow around ancient objects instead of through them.
The Rustmother believes:
The forest remembers.

She is more correct than she realizes.
The forest is connected to a fragment of the oldest environmental intelligence on Earth.
Act 9 — the first words
During the Chainscar, Kharok reconnects a major Earthworks trunk.
The Warden enters an ancient underground station.
Everything comes alive.
Lights.
Mechanical systems.
Ancient displays.
Then the lantern begins vibrating.
Yuri stops moving.
Every animal nearby becomes silent.
And through ruined speakers comes a voice.
Fragmented.
Ancient.
Barely understandable.
...HUMAN AUTHORITY... DETECTED...

Pause.

Then silence.
This should be one of those moments where players stop moving.
Because suddenly the “prophecy” isn't mythology anymore.
Something beneath them is actually talking.
The Earthwitness emerges
Do not have it appear like a random kaiju boss.
Its emergence should be terrifying, majestic, and almost sacred in tone.
During the final act, Kharok attempts full Crown synchronization through Yuri.
The network reaches critical connectivity.
For the first time since 2066, the Earthwitness has access to enough of its original systems to wake completely.
Far below the Crown—
the mountain moves.
Not explodes.
Moves.
Snow falls from cliffs.
Rock splits.
Ancient structures rise with something beneath them.
And the Warden realizes that what appeared to be part of the mountain...
was part of a body.
But don't make it look like a dragon.
It should look strange and biologically plausible.
A creature built to exist underground.
Enormous segmented forelimbs.
Stone and mineral accretion over synthetic hide.
Root-like sensory filaments.
Bioluminescent sensory pits.
Structures growing from its body that resemble both bone and machinery.
Hundreds of years of geology attached to it.
It looks less like:
monster
and more like:
a piece of Earth that decided to stand up.
And then it speaks
Not roaring.
Not telepathically.
The Earthworks awaken around it.
Every surviving speaker.
Every Crown interface.
Every functioning Road node.
Possibly even the Warden's lantern.
Its voice comes from everywhere.
And it asks something incredibly simple:
WHY HAVE YOU RETURNED TO THE CHAIN?

That line should hit hard.
Because Kharok thinks he invented the Chain.
But the Earthwitness recognizes the philosophy.
Humanity tried centralized dominion before.
Kharok has unknowingly recreated the same mistake.
Kharok's response
And Kharok should not be frightened.
He should be vindicated.
To him, this proves everything.
Humanity once controlled the Earth itself.
And he believes he is finally worthy to inherit that power.
He tells the Warden:
They commanded storms.

They moved rivers.

They made creatures such as Yuri.

They shaped the world.

And you would have us crawl through mud and call helplessness freedom?

Now Kharok's position becomes extremely compelling.
He's not trying to return humanity to primitive conditions.
He wants to restore civilization.
He sees himself as finishing what humanity started.
The Earthwitness judges neither side
This is important.
Don't make it pronounce:
“Kharok evil, Warden good.”
Its purpose isn't morality.
It's observation.
It has watched civilization rise and collapse.
It explains:
The old world failed because humans increasingly removed adaptability from systems.
Kharok's network is doing the same.
But it also warns the Warden:
Simply destroying the Crown is equally dangerous.
The modern biosphere now depends partly upon Earthworks systems.
Three centuries of adaptation have occurred.
You cannot return Earth to 2066.
The old world is gone.
This world is real now.
That gives the finale its third path.
The meaning of “Witness”
This becomes one of the most important concepts in the lore.
The Earthwitness does not rule Earth.
It witnesses Earth.
That's what humanity designed it to do.
And over three centuries, it reached an extraordinary conclusion:
The greatest mistake was confusing observation with ownership.
Humans believed understanding a system entitled them to control it.
Kharok believes exactly the same thing.
The Warden ultimately learns something different:
Understanding creates responsibility, not dominion.
That's the philosophical core.
The marking motif
There is also a very elegant way to draw loose inspiration from traditions associated with the Beast marking or distinguishing humanity without copying the religious element literally.
When the Earthwitness awakens, it emits a diagnostic signal across the Earthworks network.
Every human carrying Chain interfacing reacts.
Their Chain scars illuminate.
Some previously invisible markings appear.
Suddenly everyone can see precisely how deeply Kharok's system has entered them.
People who believed themselves free discover dormant Chain signatures.
Others discover that what they thought was a curse is simply an interface.
The event becomes known as:
The Revealing
Not because the Earthwitness morally labels people.
Because it reveals what was already there.
I think that's a much more respectful and narratively useful adaptation.
The Chainbound Host during the Revealing
This could be extraordinary visually.
Across all ten regions:
Coalpaint Raiders stop mid-charge.
Sunglass warriors drop mirrored shields.
White Teeth riders stare at glowing markings along their arms.
Fogbound Watch lanterns flicker simultaneously.
Rustwood priests see chain patterns beneath their skin.
Every Chainbound person hears the same voice:
YOU ARE BEING COMMANDED.

Imagine spending the whole game fighting these people.
And then every one of them learns simultaneously that some part of their will was never entirely their own.
That becomes the collapse of the Chainbound Host.
Kharok still refuses
This makes him tragic rather than stupid.
Kharok argues:
Of course they are commanded.

So are children.

So are soldiers.

So is every citizen beneath every law.

A world without command is merely a world awaiting the strongest monster.

And the Earthwitness answers:
THEN TEACH. DO NOT BIND.

That is the moment Kharok's entire philosophy is challenged in one sentence.
The second Wilding
Then comes the true danger.
Kharok tries to force the Crown anyway.
He overrides safeguards.
The Earthwitness warns:
SYSTEM STATE IS NON-RECOVERABLE.

2066 PARAMETERS ARE INVALID.

Meaning:
Earth in 2377 cannot survive being managed according to old-world assumptions.
Kharok is effectively telling a system designed for the old Earth to restore a world that no longer exists.
If he succeeds:
The Verdant Maw may be chemically sterilized as “invasive vegetation.”
The Sunglass Waste may trigger climate intervention.
The White Teeth may undergo forced warming.
Rustwood microorganisms may be targeted as contamination.
Worldstriders may be classified as uncontrolled bioengineered assets.
In other words:
Kharok's attempt to save civilization would destroy the civilization that actually exists.
That's an excellent final stakes escalation.
Yuri's true purpose
This also gives Yuri a magnificent final role.
Yuri's ancestral design wasn't merely transportation.
Worldstriders served as mobile ecological backups.
When environments failed, they carried viable plants, microorganisms, water ecosystems, seed stock and animal populations elsewhere.
They were literal moving arks.
That's why entire ecosystems can exist upon their backs.
And Yuri's genetic line contains something invaluable:
an ancient Earthworks protocol allowing a Worldstrider to declare:
LOCAL ECOLOGY AUTHORITATIVE
Meaning:
the environment that currently exists takes priority over outdated restoration plans.
Yuri can effectively tell the Earthworks:
Stop trying to rebuild the old Earth.

Protect this Earth.

That is what Kharok unknowingly needed Yuri for.
But instead of using him to control the world—
the Warden uses Yuri to teach the system what the world has become.
That is beautiful.
The finale
The Warden reaches the Crown.
Kharok begins the Great Stillness.
The Earthwitness rises.
The Chainbound are Revealed.
The Crown begins reverting planetary systems toward outdated 2066 environmental targets.
The world starts destabilizing.
The Warden has three apparent options:
Destroy the Crown.
Allow Kharok to control it.
Or trust the Earthwitness.
But the actual solution comes from everything learned during the journey:
the regional ecology data collected across ten acts,
Yuri's intact Worldstrider protocols,
the lantern's wayglass,
the clans' accumulated knowledge,
and the Earthwitness itself.
They restructure the Earthworks network.
Not centralized.
Not destroyed.
Distributed.
Each region becomes capable of governing its own ecological systems.
Nodes communicate.
They warn one another.
But no Crown commands them all.
And humanity can access information from the system without possessing absolute control over it.
The Chain becomes a network again.
The Roads return.
The final exchange
Kharok, watching centuries of work disappear:
Without the Chain, they will make the same mistakes.

The Warden doesn't need some huge speech.
The Earthwitness answers instead:
YES.

Pause.
AND THEY WILL BE THEIRS TO MAKE.

That line could define the whole game.
Freedom isn't presented as guaranteed goodness.
It means responsibility.
What happens to the Earthwitness afterward?
Do not kill it.
Do not make it the new ruler.
After the network is stabilized, the Earthwitness remains above ground only briefly.
Humanity sees it.
Really sees it.
The thing from their oldest legends.
The creature whose first speech preceded the fall of the old world.
And now its second speech marks the beginning of another age.
Then it begins returning underground.
Someone asks:
“Will you speak again?”
And it answers:
IF THE EARTH REQUIRES A WITNESS.

Then it disappears below.
That's it.
No exposition.
No sequel-bait villain turn.
It becomes mythology again.
Except this time...
everyone knows it was real.

Some call what happened:
The Second Speaking.
Some:
The Revealing.
Some:
The Unbinding.
Official Wilderhold records eventually use:
0 A.U. — After the Unbinding
So the game takes place exactly at the end of one historical age.
That's fitting for a story inspired partly by an eschatological sign.
Not necessarily the literal end of the world.
The end of a world.
The world of the Chain.
And the beginning of another.

They ignored it.
The network broke.

Twice mankind tried to command the Earth. Twice the Earth answered. Wilderhold is the story of whether humanity finally listens.
